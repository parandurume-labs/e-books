Part 1

Why you need this

Diagnoses the state of your company's knowledge today. Explains how RAG and an LLM Wiki differ, and sorts out the most confusing part, the difference between ontology and wiki, into four layers.

Chapter 1

Where our knowledge actually lives

Meeting notes scattered across personal notepads, product specs living only in people's heads. Diagnoses four symptoms of fragmented knowledge.
Chapter 2

A wiki, not RAG

The limits of dumping whole documents in and searching them. What changes when you structure knowledge ahead of time.
Chapter 3

Ontology and LLM Wiki are not the same thing

This one mix-up stalls projects. Sorts it out with a four-layer model and fixes eight sentences people commonly get wrong.
Chapter 4

Three tools, three jobs

Teams handles distribution, Obsidian is the window, Claude Cowork is the hands. Why this combination, and why you don't need MCP.
Part 2

Setup in 30 minutes

Install and connect everything. There's one pitfall here that causes trouble for the rest of the book if you don't clear it.

Chapter 5

Understanding Obsidian and the vault

A vault is just a folder. Why it has to be files, not a database.
Chapter 6

Sharing the vault with your team through Teams

Miss "Always keep on this device" and AI can't read the files. Covers simultaneous-edit conflicts too.
Chapter 7

Connecting the vault to Claude Cowork

Connect one folder and AI reads and writes your company's knowledge. Sorts out what works and what doesn't.
Part 3

Designing the ontology: the heart of this book

You don't copy someone else's schema. You design your own company's knowledge structure. It should take three to five hours; spend more and you're more likely to fail.

Chapter 8

Six design principles

Why tags won't do the job, the cost of over-engineering, and how to run the design meeting.
Chapter 9

Choosing your own classes

Money and a contract attached means a project; nothing attached means an opportunity. Spells out the judgment criteria.
Chapter 10

Designing relations and fields

Relation predicates, frontmatter, controlled vocabularies. The one connective thread that makes retrospectives possible.
Part 4

Running it for real

Tell AI the rules, build templates, and lock repeat work into skills.

Chapter 11

CLAUDE.md: telling AI your company's rules

One file distributes the rules to the whole team. A list of easy-to-get-wrong facts keeps hallucinations in check.
Chapter 12

Templates and your first note

Separate the source material from the processed output. One verification flag builds trust.
Chapter 13

Automating repeat work with skills

Turns structuring, retrospectives, and audits into skills. Also solves the problem of rolling them out to the team.
Part 5

Automation and visibility: hands off, eyes on

This is the hands-on part of the book. Attach the work people used to remember to a clock, make the payoff of record-keeping visible, and run 12 real work recipes.

Chapter 14

Scheduling: it runs even when people forget

The core constraints of Cowork's scheduled tasks and four workarounds. Covers Windows Task Scheduler and Power Automate too.
Chapter 15

Making work in progress visible

Gantt charts and relationship diagrams with zero plugins, Bases tables and kanban, and a dashboard that refreshes itself every week.
Chapter 16

Twelve automation recipes

From structuring meeting notes to drafting proposals. The first recipe shows everything: input, prompt, output, verification, and even the failures.
Part 6

Keeping it alive

Most knowledge-management systems die within three months. Adds two mechanisms that keep this one alive.

Chapter 17

Quality as code

"Just keep it well maintained" is not a method. Catch rule violations with a Python script instead.
Chapter 18

Comparing intent against execution

Pile up only plans and you get a planning doc. Pile up only logs and you get a diary. Only connecting the two makes it a retrospective.
Part 7

AX: from the wiki to the whole company

Adopting AI and transforming your company are not the same thing. Check where you stand with a five-stage maturity model, and get a 12-month roadmap and metrics to track.

Chapter 19

Where AX starts: the LLM Wiki

The difference between DX and AX, a five-stage maturity self-check, four rollout stages, six metrics, and five patterns behind why AX efforts fail.
