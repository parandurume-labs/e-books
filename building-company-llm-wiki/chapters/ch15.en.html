CHAPTER 15

Making work in progress visible

Design the ontology and the charts come for free

about 17 min

Once the automation from Chapter 14 is running, reports pile up. And then this happens.

In week one you read them all. In week two you read the titles. From week three you stop opening them.

That is the fate of a report made of prose. "3 ERRORs, 12 WARNs, 2 deadlines closing in" may be written down, but whether that is better or worse than last week, and where you should put your hands, does not land in one look. So reading it takes effort, and anything that takes effort ends up unread.

The real problem this chapter solves

Chapter 1 put the reason this system fails like this. "The cost of recording lands now and the benefit lands later. And that benefit is invisible."

This chapter fixes the last sentence. It makes the benefit visible. That is what keeps people recording.

First, the good news: visualization is already half done

Usually "let's build a dashboard" means bringing in a new tool, moving the data, and wiring up an integration. Here you need none of that.

Because when you designed layer 1 (the ontology) in Part 3, you already made every ingredient a chart needs.

That is the real reason not to skip Part 3. With field names unified, visualization is close to automatic; without them, no tool you buy will help. There is no way to draw a Gantt chart from 200 notes that say due yesterday, deadline today, and due_date tomorrow.

Step 1 · The nine status fields a chart needs

Chapter 10 designed your relations and fields. Here you pull out only the fields you truly need to show progress. Nine of them is enough.

FieldValuesWhat it drawsRequired

The controlled vocabulary you have to hold to here

For projects and opportunities, a status value has to be one of five: 기획 (planned), 진행 (in progress), 검토 (in review), 완료 (done), 보류 (on hold). Products use the four values from the Chapter 3 design document instead: 기획 / 개발 / 운영 / 중단. Let 「진행중」, 「진행 중」, 「진행」, and "in progress" mix and your kanban grows four columns. It does not stop at a broken picture either: ask "how many projects are in progress?" and the AI answers wrong.

So you nail it down in the layer 1 design document: status ∈ {기획, 진행, 검토, 완료, 보류}. The validation script from Chapter 17 checks it every week. A controlled vocabulary is the precondition for visualization.

Do not write progress as a percentage

You will want a field like progress: 70%. Don't. Three reasons.

  • What 70% means differs from person to person. 70% of the schedule, 70% of the deliverable, or 70% by feel.
  • It does not get updated. Some note will sit at 70% for two months. Guaranteed.
  • It stops at 90% forever. Every project does.

Use a stage instead. Define the stages per class up front and record only which one you are on.

# add to _ops/01-ontology-design.md

## Stage definitions

The 5 stages of a project (PRJ-)
1. Kickoff — contract or agreement signed
2. Design — requirements fixed
3. Build — development under way
4. Validation — pilot and testing
5. Close — final report submitted

The 4 stages of an opportunity (OPP-)
1. Found — call or lead confirmed
2. Review — decide whether to bid
3. Prepare — write the proposal
4. Submit — filed

In the note you write only stage: 3/5. Why that beats a percentage is clear. "Stage 3" can be verified. Whether the requirements are fixed is a checkable fact; 70% is a feeling nobody can check.

Step 2 · A Gantt chart with no plugins

Obsidian ships Mermaid inside it. You install nothing. The principle from Chapter 4, "we do not use plugins," holds.

You write this inside a note.

```mermaid
gantt
    title Solbit Tech, H2 2026
    dateFormat YYYY-MM-DD
    axisFormat %b
    todayMarker stroke-width:3px,stroke:#C2703C

    section Government projects
    Smart logistics R&D build   :active,  p1, 2026-03-01, 2026-08-31
    Smart logistics R&D testing :         p2, after p1, 60d
    Final report submission     :crit,    p3, 2026-11-15, 15d

    section City government call
    Proposal drafting           :active,  o1, 2026-07-25, 14d
    Submission deadline         :milestone, o2, 2026-08-08, 0d

    section Products
    Pickview beta               :         d1, 2026-09-01, 45d
    WareTwin pilot starts       :         d2, 2026-10-15, 30d
```

Rendered, it comes out like this.

Four tags are worth knowing here. active (running), done (finished), crit (critical), milestone (a point with no duration). And when you hang a task off another with after p1, the later one slips whenever the earlier one slips.

The real problem with Mermaid, and the fix

Maintain this by hand and it is out of date in three weeks

Mermaid code is not automatically wired to a note's frontmatter. Change due on the project note and the date in the Gantt chart stays as it was. You have to keep two places in step by hand.

Keeping two places in step by hand is something you will definitely stop doing. Three weeks later you have a wrong Gantt chart, which is worse than none. People believe it.

The fix is not to write it by hand. Let the scheduled task you built in Chapter 14 regenerate this code every week.

[Task name] Weekly dashboard refresh
[Frequency] Every Monday 08:00 (KST)
[Approval mode] Automatic
[Folder] Solbit Tech Wiki
[Prompt]

Read every note in Sources/20-Projects/ and rebuild _moc/000-dashboard.md.

1. Read start, due, status, and stage from the frontmatter.
2. Generate a Mermaid gantt code block.
   - status: 진행  → active tag
   - status: 완료  → done tag
   - due within 21 days  → crit tag
   - leave out notes with no start or no due, and list them separately under the table as "no dates recorded"
   - split the sections by class value
3. Below that, build a table of counts by status.
4. Below that, write "change since last week" in three lines or fewer.
   (compare with the last line of _reports/dashboard-history.csv)
5. At the very top of the file, put the generation time and this sentence:
   "This file is generated. Edit it by hand and your edit disappears next Monday."

Never do this:
- do not edit notes under Sources/
- do not infer a date. If it is missing, say it is missing

Item 5 matters. If a person writes something by hand into a generated file, it disappears the following week and that person stops trusting the system. Nail the warning inside the file.

The "no dates recorded" list turns out to be the most useful part

Show the items that did not make it into the Gantt and the gaps in your records surface on their own. "Three of our eight projects have no deadline" is a fact nobody knows without a dashboard.

The first use of visualization is not a pretty picture. It is making the empty boxes visible.

Step 3 · Finding bottlenecks with a relation map

A Gantt chart shows time. But the question you need more often at work is "why is this not moving?" You see that through relations.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    PRJ["PRJ-2025 Smart logistics
stage 3/5"] --> CAP["CAP-Object recognition
TRL 6"]
    CAP --> PRD1["Warebot
운영"]
    CAP --> PRD2["Pickview
개발"]
    OPP["OPP-2026 City call
proposal drafting"] -.used as evidence.-> PRD1
    OPP -.used as evidence.-> CAP
    PRD2 --- BLK["no pilot data
unverified"]

    style BLK fill:#FAEADF,stroke:#C2703C,stroke-width:2px
    style OPP fill:#EAF6F4,stroke:#0F766E,stroke-width:2px
```

Look at what this one picture tells you. The city call proposal leans on Warebot and object recognition as evidence, and Pickview cannot be used as evidence because there is no pilot data. Say that out loud in a meeting and it takes ten minutes; see it as a picture and it takes five seconds.

You do not write this code by hand either. The links in your notes already carry the relations, so you say "read the pdm_uses and blocked_by links under Sources/ and draw me a flowchart."

How this differs from the Obsidian graph view

Obsidian already has a graph view. It shows every link across your notes as dots and lines. It is lovely, and past a hundred notes it becomes a ball of wool. Because it shows all the links.

Mermaid is the opposite. You can pick out "only what relates to this proposal" and draw that. The graph view is for exploring, Mermaid is for explaining. For meeting material, the second wins easily.

Step 4 · Tables and kanban with Bases

Obsidian's Bases is a core feature that reads note frontmatter and shows it as a table, cards, or a list. It is core, so there is nothing to install. The data stays in the markdown, and only the way you look at it goes into a .base file.

You build _moc/project-status.base like this.

filters:
  and:
    - file.inFolder("Sources/20-Projects")
    - status != "완료"
views:
  - type: table
    name: By due date
    order:
      - due
    columns: [file.name, status, stage, owner, due]
  - type: cards
    name: By owner
    groupBy: owner

Change a value in the table and the note's frontmatter changes right away. That is what makes Bases good in daily use. You update states in one pass instead of opening notes one at a time. "Update all the project states on Friday" finishes inside two minutes.

A kanban board works the same way. Use status as the columns. As of July 2026 the kanban and calendar views for Bases are on the official roadmap, and until then a community plugin (Kanban Bases View) does the same job.

A second look at the plugin principle

Chapter 4 said "we do not use plugins." Put precisely, it is "we do not use plugins that let AI create or edit notes." They leave you managing the quality of your knowledge in two places.

A plugin that only changes the view is relatively safe. Remove it and the notes are still there. Keep one test in mind all the same. "If this plugin disappears, does my knowledge disappear?" If not, use it.

Step 5 · One weekly dashboard note

Now you gather everything so far into a single note, _moc/000-dashboard.md. It becomes the one file your team opens on Monday.

This layout follows rules.

  1. It never goes past one screen

    Once there is scrolling, the lower half goes unseen. If it looks like overflowing, cutting items is the right move.

  2. Every number carries its change

    "147" is not information; "147 ▲12" is. Direction matters more than the absolute value.

  3. Every red number carries an action

    "5 stale" on its own and nobody acts. Wire it up as "5 stale → see the list."

  4. It leaves source links

    When a dashboard number is wrong, you have to be able to tell which note to look at. Without that, people stop trusting the dashboard.

Step 6 · Keep the trend

A dashboard shows "now." But the question your CEO asks in month three is "is this getting better?" Answering that takes a trend.

In Chapter 14 you had the Python script append one line to validate-history.csv. Now you draw it. Mermaid has a simple chart too.

```mermaid
xychart-beta
    title "Reviewed notes (%)"
    x-axis [Apr, May, Jun, Jul]
    y-axis "Percent" 0 --> 100
    line [42, 58, 74, 81]
```

This one line persuades better than a page of rollout report. When it bends, you go and find out what happened that month. That is the raw material for the retrospective in Chapter 18.

When you have to show it to someone who does not use Obsidian

Your CEO may not have installed Obsidian. Then you build a single HTML file and put it in Teams. Have the Python script generate it every week.

# _ops/scripts/dashboard.py — the core part only
# read the frontmatter and render one HTML file. No external libraries.

import re, datetime
from pathlib import Path

ROOT = Path(__file__).resolve().parents[2]
rows = []
for md in (ROOT / "Sources" / "20-Projects").glob("*.md"):
    text = md.read_text(encoding="utf-8")
    fm = re.match(r"---\n(.*?)\n---", text, re.S)
    if not fm:
        continue
    meta = dict(
        re.findall(r"^(\w+):\s*\"?([^\"\n]+)\"?$", fm.group(1), re.M)
    )
    rows.append((md.stem, meta.get("status", "unset"),
                  meta.get("due", ""), meta.get("owner", "")))

rows.sort(key=lambda r: r[2] or "9999")
today = datetime.date.today().isoformat()

html = [f"<h1>Project status <small>{today}</small></h1><table>"]
for name, status, due, owner in rows:
    d_day = ""
    if due:
        left = (datetime.date.fromisoformat(due) - datetime.date.today()).days
        d_day = f"D{left:+d}"
    cls = "urgent" if d_day and left < 21 else ""
    html.append(f"<tr class='{cls}'><td>{name}</td><td>{status}</td>"
                f"<td>{due} {d_day}</td><td>{owner}</td></tr>")
html.append("</table>")

(ROOT / "_reports" / "dashboard.html").write_text(
    "<style>.urgent{background:#FAEADF}</style>" + "\n".join(html),
    encoding="utf-8")

Put that file in the Teams shared folder and it opens in one click. Then the Power Automate flow from Chapter 14 posts the link to the channel every Monday. People who know nothing about Obsidian see what this system produces. That matters more than it sounds. As the number of people who see the output grows, recording earns its keep.

What you must never measure

Read this far and a thought arrives. "We could just count how many notes each person wrote."

Don't.

Rank people by note count and the system dies

Here is exactly what happens. Note counts spike in week one. And those notes have nothing in them. One-line logs like "development proceeded today" pile up. Filling the number became the goal.

The worse part comes next. Empty notes drag down the quality of AI answers. People arrive at "we tried this and it wasn't much good." One metric kills the project.

Measure the system instead. Not the people.

Measure thisNot thisWhy
share of reviewed notesnotes written per personquality against volume. Volume is easy to game
trend in rule violations (ERROR)violations per personwhat needs fixing is the rule or the template
notes untouched for 30 days or morea staleness ranking by ownerstale usually means the owner is busy, not lazy
time it takes to write a proposalnothing herethe actual point of this system. Shrinking means success
share of "no record" answersnothing herefalling means the vault is filling up
number of people who opened the dashboardnothing herezero means this chapter's work was wasted

Watch that last metric. If nobody opens the dashboard, fix the dashboard. People are not lazy; that screen just isn't helping them.

Solbit Tech in practice: what the dashboard actually changed

For the two months before the dashboard, Solbit Tech's work log completion sat near 40%. Told to write, people wrote; left alone, they didn't.

They added one item to the dashboard. "Decisions this week." It was a box that gathered whatever the work logs had marked as a decision.

Three weeks later completion was 78%. Nobody chased anyone.

The reason came out later, in a retrospective. Park, who handles sales, put it this way. "What I wrote turned up on the screen the CEO looks at on Monday. So I started writing."

When the benefit of recording is visible, people record. No chasing was needed. What was needed was seeing where what you wrote gets used.

There was a failure alongside it. The first dashboard they built had 14 items. It took three scrolls, and nobody looked at the bottom. It has 8 now.

Try it: build the smallest dashboard

Do not aim at a finished dashboard. Start with two numbers.

  1. Count the notes in your vault. Then count the ones with confidence: reviewed. Write those two numbers into _moc/000-dashboard.md. By hand is fine. For today.
  2. Open your project and opportunity notes and check whether start and due are there. Mostly they will not be. Count how many are empty and write it on the dashboard as "no dates recorded: N".
  3. Copy the gantt example from this chapter and put in three of your own projects, no more. Check that Obsidian renders it.
  4. Now write a prompt that produces those three things and have Cowork do it once. Compare it with the one you made by hand. If they differ, work out which one is wrong. Usually the cause is an empty field in the vault.
  5. Once it comes out well, use the method from Chapter 14 to schedule it for Monday at 08:00. Get that far and this chapter is done.
  6. Two weeks later, ask yourself: "Did I open this file on Monday?" If you didn't, cut the items in half.

Quick recap

  • A report made of prose goes unread after three weeks. People record when the benefit is visible
  • A vault that will not visualize is not short of tools; its field names are all different. A controlled vocabulary is the precondition
  • Nine fields carry the charts. status · owner · start · due · stage · priority · blocked_by · updated · confidence
  • Write progress as a stage, not a percentage. "Stage 3/5" can be verified; "70%" cannot be checked
  • Draw Gantt charts and relation maps with Mermaid and no plugins. It is built into Obsidian
  • Maintain Mermaid by hand and in three weeks you have a wrong picture. Let a scheduled task regenerate it every week
  • The first use of visualization is not a pretty picture but making the empty boxes visible. Like "no dates recorded: 3"
  • Four rules for a dashboard. One screen · show the change · an action on every red number · source links
  • Nail "edit this by hand and it disappears" into any generated file
  • Do not measure notes written per person. Measure the system. Measure people and empty notes pile up
  • If nobody opens the dashboard, fix the dashboard, not the people
