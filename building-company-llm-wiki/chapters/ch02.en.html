CHAPTER 2

A wiki, not RAG

Dumping whole documents in versus structuring knowledge up front

about 14 min

After Chapter 1, a thought like this may come up.

"Can't we just make all our company documents searchable by an AI? I hear that's called RAG."

Good question. It's half right, in fact. But these two solve the same problem at different layers, and for a small organization like ours one side has the clear advantage. Here's why.

What RAG does

RAG is short for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Spelled out, it means "search, attach what you found, then write the answer."

The name is a mouthful, but the work is simple.

  1. Slice it up (chunking)

    Company documents get split paragraph by paragraph. One 100-page report becomes 500 pieces.

  2. Turn it into numbers (embedding)

    Each piece becomes a list of numbers that stands for its meaning. Sentences with similar meaning get similar numbers.

  3. Put it in a special store (vector DB)

    Those numbers go into a special store called a vector database.

  4. Pull out the similar pieces when a question arrives

    Ask "what's our revenue?" and it fetches the 5 to 10 pieces closest in meaning to that question.

  5. Hand those pieces to the AI and let it write the answer

    It passes them along with "answer using this material."

It genuinely works. At a large company with thousands of documents, this is close to the only option. Nobody can structure thousands of documents by hand.

Where it catches on a small team

Problem 1. A fragment doesn't know its context

RAG cuts documents up to use them. A cut fragment doesn't know where it came from or what was said either side of it.

The fragment cut out of the document

"It is currently at prototype stage and has no field validation to date."

The original sentence

"Competitor B's warehouse twin product is currently at prototype stage and has no field validation to date."

See the difference? From the fragment alone you can't tell whether this is about our product or a competitor's. The AI can take that fragment and answer "our product is at prototype stage."

This isn't a far-fetched example. Company documents are littered with sentences like it. Market research, competitor analysis, options you considered and dropped, assumptions and scenarios. RAG can't tell any of it apart from fact.

An incident that really happens

While a proposal was being written, the AI produced "we received the 2024 Smart Logistics Award." Checked it: no such award. A memo inside one of the documents reading "consider applying to the Smart Logistics Award call" had been sliced into a fragment and pulled in.

The sentence read smoothly, so review let it through. A sentence like that in an external document turns into a trust problem.

Problem 2. Relationships can't be expressed

RAG fetches "similar content." It can't fetch "what's connected." That difference decides everything.

Take the question "which of the technologies in Warebot came out of a government project?" To answer it you have to follow two links.

Warebot (product) └─ uses → Object recognition (capability) └─ produced by → 2025 Smart Logistics R&D project

RAG brings back fragments similar in meaning to "Warebot" and fragments similar to "government project," separately. But the line between the two isn't anywhere. So the AI has no choice but to guess.

Problem 3. Contradictions sit there quietly

Last year's document says "TRL 4," this year's says "TRL 6." RAG brings both. The AI picks a side at random. It has nothing to judge which one is current.

Worse, no person knows the contradiction exists either. An answer came out, after all.

Problem 4. Upkeep cost

You have to keep a vector DB running. Embedding API calls cost money. When a document changes you re-embed it. In a five-person company, who manages that?

What an LLM Wiki does

A different approach. Instead of trying to search well, you organize up front.

The core idea goes like this.

The core of an LLM Wiki

Rather than leaving the sources as they are and searching them, you read the sources and build notes organized in advance into "things" and "relationships between things." The AI reads finished notes, not cut fragments.

Let me pin down the scope of the name LLM Wiki here. It doesn't point at the pile of notes alone.

This book's terminology, in one sentence

LLM Wiki is the name of the whole system. The notes, the links between notes, the rules you give the AI, the check scripts, the scheduled jobs, and the team habits that keep it alive: all of it together.

Ontology, which shows up often from here on, is only one layer inside that system. It is not another name for LLM Wiki. "We built an ontology" and "we built an LLM Wiki" are entirely different sentences.

When one set of meeting minutes comes in, it gets handled like this.

Three differences that decide it

First, the context lives inside the note. "Competitor B's product is at prototype stage" goes into the competitor note. Our product note holds our product information only. They can't mix.

Second, relationships are written into the note directly. "Warebot uses object recognition" is written down in the note. The AI reads it instead of guessing.

Third, what nobody knows says "we don't know." This turns out to matter most. If the note says 확인 필요, the AI reports exactly that. It doesn't fill the blank from imagination.

Solbit Tech in practice

Solbit Tech's WareTwin note has a section called "Known limits." It reads like this.

"No field validation on a real site yet. Variation in conversion accuracy by drawing quality has not been quantified."

When a proposal is being written, the AI reads this note and does not describe WareTwin as "validated." It tells the person instead: "you need a validation case and there isn't one in the vault."

A limit written down honestly becomes the thing that prevents the accident. That is hard to do with RAG, because there is no "product note" to write the limit into in the first place.

Side by side

ItemRAGLLM Wiki
Preparation Just put the documents in (automatic) A person has to set the note format and the relationship rules first
Adding a document Re-embed. May need a full reindex Add the new note only. Existing notes stay as they are
Keeping context Cut into fragments, so context breaks Context holds at the note level
Expressing relationships Not possible (similarity only) Expressed as explicit links
Handling contradictions Brings both and the AI picks a side Records the contradiction in its own section. A person judges
Saying "we don't know" Not possible. Fills the blank with a guess Can be marked Unverified
Infrastructure needed Vector DB, embedding API None. A folder and text files
People reading it directly Hard (fragments and numbers) Easy (just documents)
Scale it suits Thousands to tens of thousands of documents Dozens to hundreds of documents
Effort Low at first, and quality is hard to improve High at first, and better as it accumulates

So when do you use which

This book is about the LLM Wiki, but it isn't saying RAG is bad. Here is how to decide.

RAG fits when

  • You have thousands of documents or more and the pile keeps growing
  • The content is already polished (laws, manuals, papers, product documentation)
  • Your goal is mostly "find it" ("where is that rule?")
  • There is nobody to do the structuring

An LLM Wiki fits when

  • You have dozens to hundreds of documents
  • The content is not polished (minutes, memos, chat screenshots, drafts)
  • Your goal is mostly "synthesize" ("pull our capabilities together")
  • Accuracy matters. This material goes into proposals, contracts, external documents
  • The knowledge is in people's heads, so a person has to pull it out either way

The call that matters

If company knowledge is already all written in documents, look at RAG.
If company knowledge is in people's heads, it's an LLM Wiki. A person has to pull it out and write it down anyway, and while writing it, giving it structure is the better move.

A company of 5 to 50 people is almost always the second case.

You can also run both

In practice this is the most realistic. Bulk documents such as laws and reference material through RAG, knowledge unique to the company in the wiki. In this book's folder structure, 90-Reference/ is where that sits.

A pile of words that all look alike

Search this topic and out come ontology, knowledge graph, taxonomy, semantic layer, one after another. The trouble is that most writing uses these words as if they were interchangeable. So you finish reading more confused than before.

Two things to settle here in advance.

First, ontology is not a synonym for LLM Wiki. Exactly as the terminology note above says. The moment you mix the two, the design work in Part 3 stops being a job you can define. This is in fact the common way an adoption goes off the rails: a beautiful design document with empty notes. The ontology is finished and the wiki hasn't started.

Second, a knowledge graph is nothing grand. One line in a note does it.

Write this inside Warebot.md pdm_uses: "[[Object recognition]]" and it means this Warebot --uses--> Object recognition That one line is one edge of a knowledge graph.

A graph forms without a dedicated database. Obsidian reads these links and actually draws the graph for you. You see it for yourself in Chapter 5.

Which layer each of these words sits on is the next chapter's whole job. It's the shortest chapter in the book, and skipping it guarantees you get stuck in Part 3.

Try it: which side is our knowledge on

  1. Roughly gauge the ratio between "already written down" and "only in someone's head" across everything your company knows. If the second is over half, an LLM Wiki is right for you.
  2. If you gave an AI some company material last month and asked it to do something, go back and check whether the result held wrong information. If it did, write down what kind of error it was. (invented fact / mixed-up context / stale information)
  3. Count how many links you have to follow to answer the question below.
    "Among the technologies in our main product, which ones were developed with government project funding?"

Quick recap

  • RAG cuts documents up, turns them into numbers, stores them, then finds the pieces similar to your question and hands them to the AI
  • RAG's weak spots on a small team: fragments don't know their context, relationships can't be expressed, contradictions sit there quietly, and it costs upkeep
  • An LLM Wiki reads the sources and builds notes organized in advance into "things" and "relationships"
  • The differences that decide it: context is kept, relationships are explicit, and you can write down "we don't know"
  • A limit written down honestly becomes the guard against hallucination
  • Knowledge in documents means RAG, knowledge in people's heads means a wiki
  • LLM Wiki is the name of the whole system, and ontology is one layer inside it. They are not synonyms
  • A knowledge graph is nothing grand. One link written in a note is one edge of the graph
