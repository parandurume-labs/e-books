If you have come this far, your company now has a few things. Several hundred structured notes, the rules and scripts that keep them, a handful of automations that run themselves every week, one dashboard that opens on Monday.

Then your CEO asks you this. "So is this our company's AI transformation?"

The accurate answer is "the foundation for it now exists, and the transformation itself starts here." This chapter is about that "here".

What AX means: the simplest version

AX comes up a lot lately. It stands for AI transformation. The fastest way to explain it is alongside DX (digital transformation), the term everyone used before it.

DX · digital transformationAX · AI transformation
What changes The tools you work with The way you decide
Example Paper approvals → electronic approvals
Notebook → Excel → groupware		 People gather the material and decide → AI gathers the material and people decide
The sign it worked The paper disappears One step of a workflow is replaced by AI
How they relate The data DX produced becomes the raw material for AX. They do not have to happen in that order, though. They can run together

The most common misunderstanding

"We brought in AI tools, isn't that AX?" No. That is the starting line.

If your people are each writing email drafts with ChatGPT or Claude, individual productivity went up. Good news, but the company has not changed. The workflow is the same, and when that person leaves it goes with them.

AX is AI entering the workflow as an official step. "When a customer enquiry arrives, AI does the first triage and drafts the reply, and people handle review and exceptions." Do that and the flow itself is different.

AX always jams at the same place

Here is the core claim of this book. Put briefly, it is this.

The one sentence for the whole book

AX does not get past the individual productivity stage not because AI is not good enough, but because there is no accurate knowledge about the company to give it.

Which is obvious once you think about it. Say you decided to have AI write the first reply to customer enquiries. To write a reply the AI has to know things like these.

  • what your product is and what it cannot do
  • what you discussed with this customer before, and what you promised
  • your pricing policy and its exceptions
  • what you answered to a similar enquiry last time

If that information sits in people's heads and personal folders, you have no way to give it to the AI. So the AI says obvious things and people conclude "this is useless". The AI did not fail. The material was not there.

To say it once more: model performance improves every year whether or not you do anything. Your company's knowledge, meanwhile, nobody will build if you do not. So which side to invest in is clear.

Why skipping from stage 1 to stage 3 fails

Plenty of companies try it. They bolt on "automatic replies to customer enquiries" or "automatic report generation". The tools work. Output really does come out.

But you cannot review it. To check whether the AI's reply is right you need the evidence, and there is none. So the reviewer ends up checking everything again, which makes it slower than before. Three weeks later that automation is switched off.

What stage 2 is worth is not getting an answer. It is "being able to see where the answer came from in 30 seconds". Stage 3 only stands on top of that.

Putting the vault on the four rollout phases

Material on AX generally lays the rollout out in four phases: Diagnose → Strategy → Pilot → Scale. The frame was written for large organizations, but a company of 5 to 50 people can use it as it is. Each phase is simply far smaller.

PhaseWhat it normally meansAt our sizeWhat the vault does
① Diagnose Assess the maturity of data, infrastructure and organizational capability Half a day. The four diagnostic questions from Chapter 1 plus the five-stage self-assessment in this chapter "Which knowledge lives only in someone's head" is the diagnosis itself
② Strategy Pick the priority initiatives by impact and feasibility, set the investment scope and the metrics Two meetings. List the repeated tasks and pick the top three With a work log you pick "what we repeat" from the record instead of guessing
③ Pilot Verify quality, cost and operability with real users and real data Four weeks. Put one recipe from Chapter 16 into real work Evidence marks let you measure quality as a number
④ Scale Standardize, embed governance Quarterly. Fix the prompts as skills and put the rules into CLAUDE.md CLAUDE.md and the ontology design doc are the governance documents

What to notice here

Phase 4, Scale, does not build a separate governance system. The CLAUDE.md and the ontology design doc you already have do that job.

Large companies spend months on AI governance because they have to turn the rules into documents and train people on them. You wrote the rules into a file the AI reads every time. Change a rule and the next conversation applies it. This is where a small organization has the advantage.

Hold the "one" in months 7 to 9

When you fold AI into a workflow, do two at once and both fail. One takes two to three months to settle, and you have to refine the review criteria over that period.

Here is how to choose. Done often, reversible when it goes wrong, and under five minutes to review. Structuring meeting notes or the weekly report make good first candidates. Contract review and quoting come later.

What you measure and report: six metrics

Around six months in you have to answer "so did it work?" Same principle as Chapter 15: you measure the system and the work, not people.

MetricHow you measure itTargetWhy this metric
1. Time to produce repeated output Compare the hours spent on proposals and weekly reports before and after Down 40% or more The only number a CEO reads. Time is money
2. Share of "no record" answers The share of AI answers where it found no evidence Falling steadily Direct proof the vault is filling up
3. Share of reviewed notes confidence: reviewed / total Held at 70% or above Stops AI output piling up unreviewed
4. Rule violations (ERROR) The weekly trend from the Chapter 17 validation script Should not rise Shows whether quality holds as the notes grow
5. Review pass rate The share of AI output that passes without edits Widen stage 3 above 80% The only basis for deciding whether to scale stage 3
6. Time for a new hire to get up to speed When they can work independently on the onboarding pack alone 3 months → 1 month The number that answers symptom 4 in Chapter 1 directly

To measure number 1 you have to measure it now

Ask "has proposal writing time come down?" six months after you start and nobody can answer. There is no number from before.

So record one thing now. "How many hours did the last proposal take in total, adding up everyone's hours." One line does it. That one line becomes your AX results report six months from now.

Five ways AX fails

1. You buy the tool first

The most common one. You sign for an AI solution, then start wondering "what data do we put in?" The order is backwards. With the knowledge organized, the tools you already have are usually enough. Notice that this book had you buy nothing new.

2. You pick an easy task so the pilot succeeds

The pilot has to succeed for the next budget, so it is understandable. But an easy task is one that never took long anyway. Succeed and there is no effect, and then there is no case for scaling.

Pick instead the work you do often, that takes a long time, and where you reassemble the same material every time. Usually proposals, reports and customer replies. Harder, but succeed here and scaling happens on its own.

3. You turn organizing knowledge into a "project"

"We will run a company-wide knowledge task force for three months." Do that and three months later you have a well-organized snapshot, and six months later it is stale. The task force is over, so nobody maintains it.

Organizing knowledge is not a project. It is a way of working. That is why this book pushes three to five minutes a day so hard. The habit of putting things back in place beats the big clear-out.

4. You build AI judgements people cannot review

Automation that produces conclusions with no evidence always gets switched off. What you cannot review you cannot be accountable for, and what you cannot be accountable for you cannot use at work.

That is why every prompt in this book carries "mark the evidence note in brackets". It is not a preference. It is a condition for AX.

5. You measure people

Chapter 15 covered this. Start counting notes per person, AI hours per person, violations per person, and you get behavior aimed at filling the number. Numbers filled that way mean nothing.

The most dangerous of the five

Number 3. The other four fail visibly, but number 3 fails while looking like a success. There is an organized wiki, so there is something to report and some praise to collect. And a year later nobody uses it.

There is a test. "When did new information last go into this system?" Two weeks or more and it is already a snapshot.

The line before stage 4, agents

Once you reach stage 3 the next question arrives on its own. "Can't we let the AI take it all the way now?"

This book does not go that far. But here are the criteria for deciding whether you can cross. All three have to hold.

Three conditions for entering stage 4

  • The review pass rate has held above 80% for three months or more. One good month means nothing. It has to survive seasonality and a change of owner.
  • There is a procedure for undoing a mistake. "If this automation sent out a wrong quote, how do we clean that up" needs an answer. Without one, not yet.
  • What was done and why stays on the record. If people only look at exceptions, the evidence behind everything that was not an exception has to be there for a later audit.

Look at the three conditions and they share something. All of them are about whether a person can check later. Not one of them is about AI performance. That is not an accident.

What to take care of in Korea as well

Once AI enters the workflow, personal data and confidential material flow with it. Two things to note.

  • The safest thing is to keep personal data out of the vault. Personal contact details for a customer's staff, CVs, HR records. If you truly need it, write only "that information is in the HR system" in the note and leave the actual values out. The confidentiality level design in Chapter 6 covers this.
  • Set things up so there is a record of what you sent the AI. Work through the vault and "which notes were used as evidence" stays in the output. Where everyone pastes into their own chat window (stage 0) there is no such record. Moving from stage 0 to stage 2 is itself a security improvement.

Some things fell short.

  • Vault use outside the CEO is still very uneven. One of the two developers barely touches it. It feels like "work outside the code" to them.
  • They revised the ontology twice. The first design was not perfect, and it took time to accept that this is normal.
  • They never reached "all our knowledge is in the vault". They probably never will. They won clearly on three axes only: proposals, customers and projects. That was enough.

That last item matters. Do not try to move everything. Win on the two or three axes where the repeat cost is highest and the system survives.

Try it: a 90-day AX plan for your company

The last exercise in the book. Thirty minutes is enough. The CEO or the knowledge owner does it.

  1. Self-assess your maturity. Read the "signs you are here" column in the five-stage table above and pick your stage. Most companies are at 0 or 1. That is normal.
  2. Write down five repeated tasks. Things you reassembled from scratch twice or more in the last three months.
  3. Write time per round × times per year next to each one. The biggest one is your pilot.
  4. Record the time now. "This task currently takes N hours in total." That one line is the baseline for your results report six months out.
  5. Write the 90-day plan in three lines.
    · Month 1: vault setup + ontology design + fill the notes that task needs
    · Month 2: apply the recipe to that task + minimum dashboard
    · Month 3: measure the time again + one scheduled job
  6. Last, decide this. The rule that "if this number has not improved in 90 days, we stop". Setting the stopping condition up front makes people far more comfortable starting.

Quick recap

  • DX changes the tools you work with, AX changes the way you decide. Using AI tools and doing AX are different things
  • AX stops at individual productivity not because of model performance but because there is no accurate company knowledge to give the AI
  • Models get better without you. Nobody builds your company knowledge but you
  • The five maturity stages are 0 everyone on their own · 1 individual productivity · 2 shared knowledge (this book) · 3 built into the workflow · 4 agents
  • Skipping 1 → 3 fails. No evidence means no review, and no review makes the work slower
  • What stage 2 is worth is not the answer but "being able to see where the answer came from in 30 seconds"
  • Across the four rollout phases (Diagnose, Strategy, Pilot, Scale), CLAUDE.md and the ontology design doc stand in for the governance documents
  • Fold in one task in months 7 to 9. Done often, reversible, under five minutes to review
  • All six metrics measure the system and the work. None of them measures people
  • The failure patterns: buying the tool first · an easy pilot · turning knowledge work into a project · automation you cannot review · measuring people
  • The three conditions for stage 4 all ask whether a person can check later
  • Do not try to move everything. Win on the two or three axes with the highest repeat cost and the system survives
